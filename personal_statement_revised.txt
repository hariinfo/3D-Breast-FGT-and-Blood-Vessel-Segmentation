As cabin lights dimmed and passengers dozed, I watched the crimson sun sink into the Atlantic while listening to Spider-Man's soundtrack. Suddenly, an announcement broke my calm: "Any doctors on board, please come to business class immediately." A passenger rushed forward and skillfully treated someone in distress. When he returned to thunderous applause, I envisioned a real-life Spider-Man with a stethoscope instead of web-shooters. Until then, I had never thought deeply about doctors, but for the rest of the flight I kept asking, "Could I become such a life-saving superhero?"

Two weeks later at my grandparents' house in India, horrific news shattered my idealism: an oncologist had been stabbed by a patient's son over a disputed diagnosis. My grandfather explained, 'This isn't unusual, Surya. Many physicians face anger and violence due to healthcare system stress.' A profession inspiring both reverence and rage—this paradox consumed me. What drives someone to dedicate their life to healing in a world that doesn't always appreciate them?

Back home, when I shared my reflections with my uncle, a radiologist, he introduced me to artificial intelligence that could ease physicians' workloads while noting their limitations, like high false-positive rates, and lack of context. This kindled my curiosity to explore technology as a solution. Initially, I considered studying biomedical engineering, believing algorithms could revolutionize healthcare from behind the scenes. But when I started working in Dr. Tim Duong's lab at Montefiore Hospital, I realized a fundamental truth: technology helps human judgment; it doesn't replace it.

While leveraging machine learning models to quantify Background Parenchymal Enhancement in breast cancer diagnosis research, I witnessed radiologists catch subtle patterns the algorithm missed. The most sophisticated AI stumbled without clinical context and understanding of the patient's history. I learned that automation could reduce physician burnout, but only human intuition could navigate the gray zones where data ends and patient care begins. As I presented my findings at my state science fair, I realized my purpose wasn't just to code or calculate — it was to translate data on screen into meaningful impact for people.

Volunteering and shadowing across clinical settings in both the US and India reinforced this conviction. I watched doctors in under-resourced communities perform miracles with limited technology, their empathy bridging gaps that equipment couldn't fill. Conversely, I saw state-of-the-art facilities where technology enhanced—but never substituted for—the human connection.

The physician I saw on that flight wasn't heroic because of a tool—he was heroic because he'd spent years preparing to show up when needed, accepting both society's gratitude and its occasional hostility. All these contrasting experiences have progressively deepened my self-awareness: becoming a physician transcends my childish desire—it's an unwavering commitment to addressing healthcare inequities while improving human life. Medicine is more than mere admiration — it is my conviction. I no longer see doctors as superheroes who just rescue people in moments of crisis; I see them as scientists, advocates, and teachers who use both intellect and empathy to improve lives. I still carry forward my interest in leveraging technology, but it now serves my larger purpose: becoming a physician who can integrate technology, equity, and humanity into care. The journey from awe to awareness has shown me that true heroism lies not in dramatic rescues, but in consistent service — in striving, every day, to make someone else's life better.
